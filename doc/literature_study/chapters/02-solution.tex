\chapter{Solution approaches}
\label{solution}
As explained before Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), the transcription of spoken words to their textual representation, is an important task for many modern applications, like voice assistants or other speech interfaces.
Therefore lot of solutions and different approaches have been developed througout the years, a few of which were already mentioned in \autoref{intro}, since the idea gained more attention in the 1950s.

The very first systems started using a deterministic modelling of distinct phonemes or formant patterns to distinguish a relatively small set of unique reference speech signals.
Their main focus layed on digits, math operations and single letters, like \textit{Audrey} by Bell Laboratories \cite{moskvitch2017machines} or \textit{IBM Shoebox} \cite{ibmhistory}.
This type of systems stayed popular for a long time and was promoted by some influencial players like IBM and the Bell Laboratories, who aimed for comercial use cases in office correspondence and simple voice command controls.
Despite the limited capabilities of these first systems, they were considered as a breakthrough in human-machine interaction \cite{ibmhistory}.

Throughout the 1970s there were improvements in recognizing limited vocabularies and especially single words, but the next important step was caused by the shift of focus to statistical modelling of continuous speech inputs using Hidden Marcov Models (HMMs), which became popular in the 1980s \cite{kincaid2018history}.
These HMM-based systems were the first that had a real benefit for speaker-unspecific recognition use cases and could work with a relatively large vocabulary.
